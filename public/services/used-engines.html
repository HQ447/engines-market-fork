What Is a Used Engine-

Understanding the used engine market

A used engine is a complete engine removed from a donor vehicle - typically a write-off, scrapped car, or vehicle that has been retired for reasons other than engine failure. The engine is visually inspected, compression tested, and sold as-is.

What "Tested" Actually Means

✅ Typically includes:
  • Compression test performed on all cylinders (numbers available on request)
  • Visual inspection for external damage, leaks, and wear
  • Cold start video available (on request from reputable suppliers)
❌ Does NOT mean:
  • The engine has been dismantled and inspected internally
  • Internal components have been replaced
  • The engine has been run under load

Warranty Guide

Warranty - What You Get and What You Don't

Used engines typically come with a limited warranty - shorter than reconditioned units but better than nothing.

Warranty LengthTypical CoverageTypical ExclusionsBest For
30 daysParts only (internal mechanical failure)Labour, ancillaries, gaskets, sealsBudget-conscious, short-term use
3 monthsParts only (internal mechanical failure)Labour, ancillaries, gaskets, sealsMost used engine buyers
6 monthsParts only (internal mechanical failure)Labour, ancillaries, gaskets, sealsHigher-value vehicles, extra peace of mind
✅ What the Warranty Covers (Typically)
  • Internal mechanical failure of the engine assembly (block, cylinder head, crankshaft, pistons, connecting rods, camshaft, valves, timing components)
❌ What the Warranty Does NOT Cover (Typically)
  • Labour costs
  • Ancillaries (alternator, starter, turbo, injectors, fuel pump)
  • Belts and hoses
  • Gaskets and seals (if they fail after installation)
  • Damage caused by incorrect fitting, inadequate lubrication, or overheating after installation
  • Damage caused by neglect or failure to maintain
The Golden Rule:

Always read the warranty terms before committing. A supplier who offers a "warranty" but cannot tell you the terms, duration, or claims process is not offering a warranty - they are offering two words on a listing.

Questions to Ask About Warranty

  • 1. What exactly does the warranty cover- (Parts only- Parts and labour-)
  • 2. What is the duration- (30 days- 3 months- 6 months-)
  • 3. What is the claims process- (Who do I contact- What documentation do I need-)
  • 4. What are the exclusions- (Gaskets- Seals- Ancillaries-)
  • 5. Is the warranty transferable if I sell the vehicle-
Risks

Used Engine Risks & Caveats

Used engines are the cheapest option - but they carry the highest risk. Here's what you need to know.

Risk 1: Unknown Service History
A used engine comes from a donor vehicle. You rarely know how it was maintained. Was the oil changed regularly- Was the correct oil used- Was the timing chain ever replaced- The answers are usually "unknown."
Mitigation: Ask for donor mileage and, if possible, service history. Look for lower mileage and documented history.
Risk 2: No Upgraded Components
A used engine of the same type carries the same design flaws as the one that failed. If your BMW N47 failed due to a timing chain issue, a used N47 of the same age may have the same vulnerability.
Mitigation: If your engine has a known design flaw, consider whether a used engine is the right choice. A reconditioned engine with upgraded components addresses the root cause.
Risk 3: Potential Hidden Damage
A used engine may have internal damage that is not visible during a compression test. Cracks in the block, worn bearings, or damaged cylinder bores may not be detected until the engine is installed and running.
Mitigation: Ask for a cold-start video and compression figures. Buy from a reputable supplier with a clear returns policy.
Risk 4: Parts Availability
For older or less common engines, parts may be difficult to source. If the used engine you buy requires replacement of ancillaries, you may struggle to find them.
Mitigation: Check the availability of common replacement parts before committing.
Risk 5: Fitting Costs Are Not Recoverable
If a used engine fails, the warranty typically covers the engine unit only - not the labour to remove and refit it. You could pay £2,000 for fitting, only to pay it again if the engine fails.
Mitigation: Buy from a supplier with a warranty that covers labour (rare for used engines). Otherwise, budget for the possibility of repeat labour costs.
Risk 6: No Emissions Guarantee
A used engine may not meet the emissions standards of your vehicle's original engine. This could cause MOT failures or compliance issues in ULEZ / clean air zones.
Mitigation: Ensure the engine code (including suffix) matches your original engine exactly. Check the emissions classification before buying.
Supplier Checklist

Choosing a Used Engine Supplier

Ask these 8 questions to every used engine supplier before you commit.

1. "What are the compression figures - per cylinder-"
A proper compression test produces numbers, not assurances. Healthy petrol engines should read 150-180 psi per cylinder; diesels read higher. Consistency across all cylinders is what matters most. Ask for the actual numbers.
2. "What exactly is included-"
Specifically: turbocharger, alternator, starter motor, injectors, fuel pump, engine wiring harness, and all sensors. One supplier's "complete engine" may include everything; another's may be a bare long block. The difference can exceed £600.
3. "What was the donor vehicle - and why was it scrapped-"
An engine from a rear-ended 45,000-mile car is a very different proposition from one out of a 140,000-mile fleet disposal. Both can be viable, but the price and remaining service life should reflect the history.
4. "What warranty is included - and what exactly does it cover-"
3-6 months is standard for used engines. "Supplied with warranty" - with no terms, no duration, and no claims procedure - is not a warranty. It is two words on a listing.
5. "Is there a video of this specific engine running from cold-"
A cold-start video - no knocking, no smoking, smooth idle - is the closest thing to hearing the engine yourself before installation. Not every supplier records one, but those who do are demonstrating confidence.
6. "Does the engine code match my vehicle exactly - including the suffix-"
A "BMW N47" could be one of dozens of variants. The suffix determines turbo type, injectors, emissions class, and ECU compatibility. The supplier should confirm the full code before you pay.
7. "What should I check when the engine arrives-"
A reputable supplier will tell you to photograph the pallet before unwrapping, inspect for transport damage, verify the engine code stamped on the block, and check that all listed ancillaries are physically present - before signing for the delivery.
8. "What do I need to do about the DVLA and my insurer-"
The supplier should be able to tell you that the DVLA must be notified of the engine number change via the V5C [DVLA, 2025], and that you should check with your insurer - some policies require notification of major mechanical changes, while others may not.
Delivery Checklist

Delivery Day - What to Inspect Before You Sign

A used engine is a heavy, dense object shipped on a wooden pallet. It can shift during transit. These five steps take approximately ten minutes and can save weeks of dispute.

1
Photograph the Pallet - Before You Unwrap Anything
Take clear photos of all four sides and the top while the engine is still wrapped. These images establish the condition of the delivery as it arrived. If the pallet is visibly damaged - broken wood, crushed corners, leaning - photograph the damage in detail.
2
Unwrap and Photograph Again
Remove the wrapping. Photograph the engine from every accessible angle - front, rear, both sides, and top. Use a torch or your phone's flash if the light is poor. Look for anything that was not visible through the wrapping.
3
Check for Visible Damage
Look specifically for: cracks in castings (block, head, sump), dents in the sump or timing cover, broken or bent sensors, and any exposed ports. If the engine has been dropped or mishandled, the evidence will usually be visible.
4
Verify the Engine Code
Locate the engine code stamped on the block or printed on the engine's sticker. Compare it to the code on your order documentation - including the suffix. If they do not match, do not sign. Contact the supplier immediately.
5
Check That All Listed Ancillaries Are Present
Refer to the written confirmation you received when you asked "What exactly is included-" Check that the turbocharger, alternator, starter motor, injectors, fuel pump, engine wiring harness, and all sensors are physically present. If anything is missing, photograph the gap and note it on the delivery document before signing.
If anything is damaged or missing:

Note it clearly on the delivery document before you sign. Take additional photographs of the specific issue. Contact the supplier immediately.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about used engines

How much does a used engine cost in the UK- ×
Based on 7,284 used engine quote requests in 2025 [EM-VERIFIED], used engines cost £1,500-£2,800 supply only or £2,500-£4,500 fitted. Prices vary by engine type, mileage, and regional labour rates.
Is a used engine worth it for an older car- +
In most cases, yes. If your car is worth £2,000-£4,000 and the alternative is scrapping it and buying a replacement vehicle with unknown history, the arithmetic favours the used engine. A £1,500-£2,500 engine with fitting represents a total investment of £2,500-£4,500 - typically far less than the depreciation and uncertainty of buying a replacement car.
What's the typical mileage of a used engine- +
Used engines typically have between 40,000 and 100,000 miles, depending on the donor vehicle. Lower mileage engines command a premium. Always ask the supplier for the exact mileage of the donor vehicle.
What warranty comes with a used engine- +
Used engines typically carry a 1-6 month warranty covering parts only (internal mechanical failure). Labour is not covered. Always read the warranty terms before committing.
Why are used engines cheaper than reconditioned- +
Used engines are sold as-is - no internal parts are replaced, and the service history is often unknown. Reconditioned engines are fully dismantled, cleaned, inspected, and rebuilt with new parts, which adds significant cost.
Can I fit a used engine from a different vehicle- +
Yes, but you need the exact same engine code and often the same variant. Your supplier will match the engine code to your vehicle's VIN. Never assume a similar engine will fit.
What should I ask a used engine supplier before buying- +
  1. Compression figures per cylinder.
  2. Donor vehicle mileage and reason for scrappage.
  3. Cold-start video.
  4. Exact engine code (including suffix).
  5. Warranty terms.
  6. What ancillaries are included.
Do I need to tell the DVLA and my insurer- +
DVLA - update the engine number on your V5C [DVLA, 2025]. Insurer - you should check with your insurer. Some policies require notification of major mechanical changes, while others may not.
